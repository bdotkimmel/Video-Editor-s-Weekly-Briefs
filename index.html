B133 · The throat-clearing that keeps coming back

Production directions

Story: Keep the repeated throat-clearing, swallowing and morning frustration central. Don’t turn the ending into generic sleep, cold prevention or a spray-dependency story.

Anatomy: Use a clear side-view connection from nose to throat. Show normal drainage, then more noticeable mucus with irritation. Don’t show all drainage forcibly reversing because the nose is “sealed shut.”

Voice: A warm adult voice, understandable for a 55+ viewer. Leave room around the relatable throat-clearing moment; avoid a clinical lecture.

Customer evidence: If Chris JM’s quote is used, show his actual saved comment. A narrator reads an attributed quote; an actor mustn’t present it as their own outcome.

Core desire: Less throat clearing and fewer interruptions from post-nasal drip

Who: Adults who keep swallowing or clearing mucus at the back of their throat, especially in the morning, and have already tried remedies. Target the nasal-allergy/post-nasal-drip branch—not reflux or every unexplained cough.

Awareness: Solution aware about recurring drip and familiar remedies; unfamiliar with nasal light therapy. No option assumes they’re shopping for a light device.

Angle: The throat is where you feel it; the nose can be where it starts. Explain that connection, then introduce targeted nasal light as an approach to try for the allergy-related irritation behind the drip.

Why we’re targeting this

Chris JM specifically reported that he didn’t have to clear his throat as much. Other internal reviews describe buying for a partner’s post-nasal drip. External accounts repeatedly describe the stuck-mucus feeling, swallowing and morning clearing.

The nose normally makes mucus that’s swallowed unnoticed. Extra or thick mucus can become noticeable as drainage and throat clearing. Allergies are one cause; the product argument stays with that branch.

B134 · I’ve tried enough things. Why would light be different?

Production directions

Delivery: A realistic, calm presenter in their late 50s or 60s. No doctor costume or invented medical credentials. Sound like you’re answering a reasonable question.

Proof: Make the relevant research easy to read. Label the 1997 result as nasal-allergy research; don’t turn 70%/3% endoscopic improvement into a NovaFlow success rate.

Demonstration: Show actual insertion, one button and automatic completion. Don’t use a glowing product shot as proof of a health outcome.

Core desire: Confidence that a different approach is worth trying

Who: An adult—especially 55+—who’s tried several congestion remedies and doesn’t want another disappointing purchase. They’re familiar with remedies, but nasal light sounds strange or unconvincing.

Awareness: Solution aware and skeptical; not assumed to know NovaFlow or to be comparing light-device specifications.

Angle: Answer “why would this be different?” with a clear mechanism, applicable research or a visible practical comparison, then give them time to judge it for themselves.

Why we’re targeting this

Paul Yarwood asks whether someone who purchased can confirm it actually works. Chris JM and Chad West describe skepticism; Gina describes having exhausted options. These are objections to another disappointing purchase, not a need for an older actor alone.

The 1997 placebo-controlled nasal-allergy trial gives one concrete research result to explain; it tested a different device and protocol, not NovaFlow.

Offer: Use the verified current two-device offer only if it remains available at final export. Otherwise use the offer-neutral 120-day guarantee close.

Core desire: More comfortable nasal breathing for each person, with less household hassle
